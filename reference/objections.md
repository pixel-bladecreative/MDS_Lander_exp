# Objections — What They Actually Mean and What To Say

The rule underneath all of these: **an objection is a question wearing a
disguise.** "I need to think about it" is almost never about thinking. Your job
is to find the real question without making the person feel caught.

Never argue. Never "overcome." Answer the actual question and the objection
dissolves on its own.

---

## Product objections

### "The gas mileage isn't great."
*The real question: am I going to regret this every time I fill up?*

This is the #1 objection on a CX-5 and it is legitimate. Do not deflect.

> "You're right, and I'd rather do the math with you than talk you out of it.
> How many miles do you drive a year? … At [X] miles, the difference between
> this and a RAV4 hybrid is about $[Y] a month in gas. The 0% financing is
> worth more than that over the term — and that's money you keep no matter
> what gas does. But if you're driving 25,000 a year, I'll be honest, the
> hybrid math flips. Let me show you the CX-50 Hybrid before you decide."

Losing a CX-5 deal to your own CX-50 Hybrid is a win. Losing it to a RAV4
because you argued is not.

### "I'll wait for the CX-5 hybrid next year."
*The real question: am I buying the about-to-be-outdated one?*

> "That's a fair instinct. Two things worth knowing: first-year builds of a new
> powertrain are the ones I'd personally wait on, not the one that's been
> refined. Second, launch cars don't carry incentives — right now there's 0% on
> the table, and that goes away the moment demand shows up. You'd likely pay
> more next year for the privilege of going first."

### "The third row is too small." (CX-90)
*The real question: will my family actually fit?*

Qualify instead of defending:

> "Is the third row an everyday thing, or occasional — extra kid, carpool,
> grandparents twice a year? … If it's everyday with luggage, I'll tell you
> straight, the Telluride has more room back there and you should look at it.
> If it's occasional, put your seat where you like it and climb in the back —
> I'll wait. Most people find it's the second row and how it drives that they
> live with every day."

### "Isn't Mazda more expensive?"
It usually isn't, and this one is just a fact:

> "That's the reputation, but the CX-5 starts about $1,900 under a RAV4 and
> $900 under a CR-V — with all-wheel drive standard, which both of them charge
> extra for. It looks expensive. It doesn't price expensive."

### "How's the reliability compared to Toyota?"
Do not oversell:

> "Toyota's reputation is earned, I won't pretend otherwise. What I'd point you
> to is that nearly the whole Mazda line took IIHS Top Safety Pick+ for 2026 —
> that's an independent lab, not a marketing claim. And the powertrain in this
> is naturally aspirated and about as simple as it gets. Here's the warranty in
> writing; compare it side by side."

## Process objections

### "I'm just looking."
*Translation: don't pounce on me.*

> "Good — you should be. I'm not going to follow you around. I'm [NAME], the keys
> are inside if you want to drive anything, and if you'd rather just wander,
> that's completely fine."

Then actually leave them alone. This one move separates you from every
salesperson they have ever met.

### "I need to think about it."
*Translation: there's an unanswered question I haven't said out loud.*

> "Absolutely. Can I ask — is it the car, the numbers, or the timing? Whichever
> it is, I'd rather you think about the right thing than the wrong one."

Nearly always it is the numbers, and nearly always they have not told you the
real constraint yet.

### "I need to talk to my spouse."
Legitimate, and never a brush-off to fight.

> "Of course. What are they going to ask you that you don't have an answer for
> yet? Let's get that handled now so you're not stuck fielding it alone."

### "Your price is higher than [other dealer]."
Never bash the other store.

> "They might well have a better number — send me the quote and I'll tell you
> honestly if I can touch it. What I'd check is whether it's the same trim and
> whether the fees are in it, because that's usually where the difference lives.
> If they've genuinely got you a better deal, I'll tell you to take it."

You will lose a few and win far more, and the ones you win will send you
referrals for years.

## Money objections

### "That payment is too high."
Never immediately jump to a longer term. Find the real number first:

> "What were you hoping to be at? … Okay. Let me show you three ways to get
> closer, and I'll tell you the trade-off on each one — because some of these
> cost you money later and I'd rather you know which."

### "My trade is worth more than that."
*The real question: are you lowballing me?*

> "Tell me what you're basing it on — I want to see the same numbers you're
> seeing. Sometimes it's a trim or an option package we missed, and that's a
> real correction. Sometimes it's a private-party number, which is a fair
> number but it's a different transaction. Either way let me show you exactly
> how we got there."

Transparency on the appraisal is worth more than the $500 you might hold.

### "The rate is too high."
> "What tier did they put you in? … Let me have the finance manager look at
> whether we can get you into a better bucket, and let's see if the factory
> rate beats your credit union — sometimes it does and sometimes it doesn't.
> I'll tell you which."

## The follow-up objection nobody teaches

### They ghost you.
Not an objection — an information gap. They bought elsewhere, they got scared,
or life happened. One honest message beats ten chirpy check-ins:

> "Hey [name] — no pitch here. Just wanted to make sure you landed somewhere
> good. If you already bought, genuinely congratulations, tell me what you got.
> If you're still looking, I'm around."

The reply rate on that message is remarkable, and it is the one people
remember six months later when their sister needs a car.
